# Research: Taylor Swift — Songwriting Formula (folklorian lane: folklore/evermore → Midnights → re-recordings → TTPD)

**Lane:** folklore/evermore (2020), "All Too Well (10 Minute Version)" (2021), Midnights (2022), Speak Now TV (2023), 1989 TV (2023), THE TORTURED POETS DEPARTMENT (2024)
**Distinct angle:** the most commercially consistent songwriter of her generation with unusually well-documented songcraft discipline
**Seam:** narrative-detail method (notes-app idea lists, "rant bridges," third-section reveals, concrete nouns), co-writer rotation (Dessner / Antonoff / earlier Max Martin+Shellback), conversational midrange topline

## Summary

Taylor Swift's repeatable hit engine is lyric-first narrative craft executed on a tight, repeatable production loop with two long-term co-writers: write the complete top line (words + melody) over a supplied instrumental (Dessner's voice-memo/file workflow) or in an in-person "organic" session (Antonoff's studio workflow), anchor every song in concrete nouns and named places, build verses as setup and make the bridge the emotional crescendo ("rant bridge"), and sing the hook in a flat, conversational midrange that reads as confession rather than performance. The formula is documented in her own words across Rolling Stone's McCartney conversation (2020), the April 2026 New York Times Magazine interview, and her co-writers' accounts; its commercial output between 2020 and 2025 is unmatched: 12 Hot 100 #1s (6 since 2020), the first top-10 sweep of the Hot 100 (Midnights), the first top-14 sweep (TTPD), the longest song ever to top the chart ("All Too Well (10 Minute Version)", 10:13), and the biggest streaming week in album history (TTPD, 891.34M U.S. on-demand streams in week one).

**Verification note:** Primary quotes below were fetched from archived primary sources (Rolling Stone interviews via Wayback Machine, NYT via partial transcript + USA Today's same-interview reporting, Variety, Billboard). One widely-circulated legend — a Notes-app folder called "Bright Blue" — could **not** be verified in any primary source; see Gaps.

## 2) The core repeatable workflow/formula — as she actually does it (documented step-by-step)

Synthesized from primary interviews (RS McCartney 2020; RS Dessner 2020 ×2; Variety 2024; Billboard 2023; NYT 2026):

1. **Collect fragments constantly, never in the studio.** She keeps a running file of words, questions, phrases and song titles in her phone's Notes app; she does not bring social media into the recording studio, and "when she's in her Notes app, 'it looks like I'm just endlessly scrolling, but I'm scrolling through words in my file.'" (NYT via USA Today, Apr 2026). In 2020 she confirmed she keeps "lists and lists" of favorite words ("elegies," "epiphany," "divorcée"). (RS McCartney, Nov 2020)
2. **Hook/title-first ideation.** Song ideas arrive as images and titles ("It started with imagery. Visuals that popped into my mind…" — folklore primer, Billboard, Jul 2020). She carries hooks and titles until an instrumental or moment activates them; e.g., the entire 10-minute "All Too Well" began as an improvised rant over four chords at a Speak Now tour rehearsal (2011), recorded by a sound engineer, later reconstructed from diaries and notes (NYT 2026; Tonight Show via Wikipedia; RS 500 Greatest Albums podcast via Songfacts).
3. **Two production lanes, same division of labor:**
   - **Dessner lane (remote, instrumental-first):** Dessner sends folders of instrumentals; Swift writes the **entire top line (melody + lyrics) and returns a voice memo within hours**; Dessner doesn't know the title, subject, or where the chorus goes until it arrives. "Hours after, she sent back a fully written version of 'Cardigan.' It was like a lightning bolt struck the house." (RS, Jul 2020). Same for "willow" (written in ~an hour over Dessner's "Westerly" sketch) and "tolerate it" (written over a 10/8 instrumental Dessner almost didn't send). (RS, Dec 2020)
   - **Antonoff lane (in-person, organic):** "It's always an off-the-cuff surprise — dinner at the house that turns into messing around in my little studio that turns into a new idea. It's utterly organic… like two people flying a plane." (Variety, Dec 2024). Midnights was recorded at Antonoff's Rough Customer (Brooklyn) + Electric Lady; she co-wrote 11 of 13 standard tracks with him. (Wikipedia: Midnights)
4. **Lyrics first, meaning before sound.** Dessner: "she was inspired to write something until she really knew what it was about" — she narrates each song's story to the co-writer before/while writing. (RS, Jul 2020)
5. **Write fast, finish late.** Standard drafts come quickly; she habitually writes one or two **final songs days before mastering** ("the 1" and "hoax" for folklore; "happiness" and "right where you left me" for evermore). Dessner: "That is a little bit how she works — she writes a lot of songs, and then at the very end she sometimes writes one or two more, and they often are important ones." (RS, Dec 2020)
6. **Bridge as the climax, engineered to resolve.** The bridge is where the song "zooms back" 20 feet to reveal the whole painting (NYT 2026). With Antonoff she formalized the **"rant bridge"**: stream-of-consciousness, intrusive thoughts, crescendo — exemplified by "Out of the Woods," "Cruel Summer," "Is It Over Now?," "Hits Different," "The Smallest Man Who Ever Lived." (NYT 2026)
7. **Third-section reveal / callback payoff.** Storyteller structure learned from country narrative songs: hook repeats with **changed meaning** as the narrator ages; close by bringing back the song's first line ("if you really want to get me to cry, bring back that same first line of the song and end the song with it" — NYT 2026). In "All Too Well" the scarf planted in verse 1 is paid off in the final verse (Chekhov's-gun detail; The Atlantic/Pitchfork analysis via Wikipedia).
8. **Remove the "radio parameters."** folklore/evermore were made by explicitly dropping the old filters ("How will this song sound in a stadium?… on radio?") — and then performed *better* commercially than the parameter-driven records. (RS McCartney, Nov 2020)
9. **Keep the process secret until release.** folklore and evermore were written in total secrecy (Dessner: collaborators didn't know who they were working for), which Swift credits as necessary "creative freedom." (RS, Jul 2020)

**Why this yields consistent chart + cultural dominance:** the loop is not dependent on any single producer's taste (two interchangeable lanes), it front-loads the durable asset (words) rather than the perishable one (beat), and every song ships with built-in Easter-egg/detail hooks that generate re-listen culture (lyrics-as-puzzle; "I created character arcs and recurring themes that map out who is singing about who" — folklore, RS via Wikipedia).

## 3) Lyric method — theme/story/rhyme/hook generation, bridge discipline, noun specificity

- **Story/time structure (her own doctrine):** "first verse, a little girl learns a lesson that, in the chorus, her mom teaches her about. Then the little girl grows up… the second time you hear the hook, that same hook means something a little bit different because she's grown up… Then the bridge, maybe she goes on in her life… And if you really want to get me to cry, bring back that same first line of the song and end the song with it." (NYT, Apr 28, 2026) — the verified version of the "third-verse plot twist" (see Gaps).
- **Specificity of nouns/names/places — the core device.** "All Too Well" is built on the scarf, the refrigerator light, "your sister's house," the twin-sized bed, "dancing 'round the kitchen"; the 10-minute version adds the age-gap taunt, the 21st-birthday party, the "fuck the patriarchy" keychain. "There's just enough detail in Swift's songwriting to give her story the ring of truth." (Vox, Nov 2021). folklore is named-place-heavy: "The Last Great American Dynasty" (Rebekah Harkness of Watch Hill, Rhode Island), "the High Line," "Guadalcanal." Her stated rationale: she retains "that level of detail and intensity" of feeling from ages 17–22 ("You notice candle ash on the cuff of the shirt, and the button…"). (NYT 2026)
- **Character work.** folklore/evermore = fictional narrators with interlocking arcs (the teenage love triangle: "cardigan"/"august"/"betty"); "Clara Bow" ends with a new girl who "looks like Taylor Swift" — "I want to be able to put my own image on these characters." (NYT 2026). TTPD's "Fortnight" = a suburban what-if: "You ended up not with the person that you loved and now you just have to live with that every day." (Amazon Music, via Wikipedia)
- **Word-level rules (stated 2026):** loves alliteration ("a chef's kiss to two back-to-back words having the same letter"); avoids a word ending with the same letter the next word starts with — example: changed "and you talk real low" to "talk real slow" in "Our Song" so the 's' breaks up the 'l's. (NYT via USA Today)
- **Juxtaposition/polarity:** "I really gravitate towards juxtaposition and polarity in a line… we are all filled with polarity, hypocrisy, these kind of battling features…" — e.g., "the girl who has everything and nothing all at once" ("Elizabeth Taylor"), "our coming of age has come and gone" ("peace"). (NYT 2026)
- **Bridge discipline (the "rant bridge"):** defined as "basically stream of consciousness, endless pouring out of emotion, intrusive thoughts blended with metaphor, with discussion, with shouting" ("Out of the Woods," "Is It Over Now?," "Cruel Summer"); engineered to "feel the most intense of what that feeling is that you're trying to establish… and you want it to kind of be a crescendo." (NYT 2026)
- **Hook generation:** hooks are conversational confessions with a self-aware twist ("It's me, hi, I'm the problem, it's me"; "I love you, it's ruining my life") — singable plain speech, not melodic scat. "All Too Well" itself began as a rant; "No Body, No Crime" as a solo voice-memo on a rubber-bridge guitar (RS Dec 2020).
- **Word lists:** "I have favorite words, like 'elegies' and 'epiphany' and 'divorcée,' and just words that I think sound beautiful, and I have lists and lists of them." (RS McCartney, Nov 2020)

## 4) Topline/singing method — melodic construction, phrasing, conversational midrange, how the hook is SUNG

- **The top line is the song.** In the Dessner lane she owns melody + lyrics outright: "I would write the entire top line for a song, and he wouldn't know what the song would be about, what it was going to be called, where I was going to put the chorus." (RS McCartney, Nov 2020)
- **Conversational midrange as default.** Critics consistently clock the same device across eras: NPR's Ann Powers on Midnights — Swift's vocals keep a country-influenced "relaxed" timbre and "interpolated hip-hop cadences that resulted in conversational sing-talking" (via Wikipedia: Midnights); on TTPD she "sings mostly in the lower ranges of her vocal register, resulting in a rap-like, conversational delivery" (Ann Powers, NPR review, Apr 19, 2024, cited in Wikipedia: TTPD). Music professor Samuel Murray notes her recurring "one-note melodies and a recitative singing style" (via Wikipedia: TTPD).
- **The "bisected" phrase.** The New Yorker's Lauren Michele Jackson on "Anti-Hero": verses are "sung aloft and lilting on one phrase and near monotonic the next" — each confession followed by a near-spoken rebuttal (via Wikipedia: Anti-Hero).
- **The hook is SUNG flat, on repeated pitches.** "Fortnight": Swift's verses "sung in a monotone" over a pulsing eighth-note synth bass, with the hook line "I love you, it's ruining my life" carried by lead + Malone's echoing harmony (Wikipedia: Fortnight). "Anti-Hero" sits in E major at 97 BPM with a LinnDrum/tremolo loop; the chorus hook is delivered in the same flat, talk-sung register that made "It's me, hi" a meme-recitable line. "Cruel Summer" (A major, 170 BPM) spans A2–E5 but its signature moments are the spoken-shriek bridge ("He looks up, grinning like a devil") and the "ooooh" hook (Wikipedia: Cruel Summer).
- **Phrasing as obsession.** "She obsesses over phrasing, rhythm and contradiction in her verses before unleashing… the bridge" (NYT via USA Today, 2026). Her recorded delivery runs on syllabic density — words packed into speech-rhythm lines — rather than melisma; Caramanica (NYT) highlights her "question-mark syllables" and "hard-felt smears" (via Wikipedia: Cruel Summer).
- **The voice as human signal, not instrument.** Dessner: "The warmth, humanity and raw energy of her vocals, and her writing on this record, from the very first voice memos — it was all there." (RS, Jul 2020). Anti-Hero's final choruses are "out of breath" (Variety, via Wikipedia) — the exhaustion is part of the performance.

## 5) Vocal-anchoring production — what Dessner and Antonoff do around her voice

- **Dessner's explicit brief: keep the voice human.** "We really wanted to keep her voice as human, and kind of the opposite of plastic, as possible. That was a bit of a battle. Because everything in pop music tends to be very carved out… as pushed as possible so that it translates to the radio." (RS, Jul 2020). Result: voice "very much front and centre and high in the mix, and generally sounds fairly dry," retaining a "warmth… very bright and cut really well on the radio"; several final mixes were the original unedited mixes — willow "settled back almost to the point where it began." (Sound on Sound profile, via Wikipedia: Evermore)
- **Recording intimacy:** evermore vocals were tracked in person at Long Pond on Dessner's Telefunken mic/Siemens preamps; her vocals are layered but dry-centered, with quiet, breathy, conversational tone. (Wikipedia: Evermore; Sound on Sound)
- **Antonoff's loop:** 1980s analog synths (DX7, OB-8, Juno 6, Korg M1) + LinnDrum/tremolo drums, multitracked ("digitally manipulated") vocals, pitched-down low-register doubles on tracks like "Midnight Rain" — but always the vocal at center; "Swift's words, vocals, and Antonoff's production emphasize rhythms." (Wikipedia: Midnights). For "Anti-Hero" he built on "Out of the Woods" and used the OB-8 for an "old organ in a baseball stadium" tone (Time, via Wikipedia).
- **Mixers/engineers in both lanes:** Serban Ghenea mixes (Fortnight, Cruel Summer, folklore tracks); Laura Sisk records her vocals (Electric Lady/Kitty Committee); TTPD vocals recorded by Sisk and Christopher Rowe; Dessner's long-time engineer Jonathan Low mixes at Long Pond. (Wikipedia: Fortnight, TTPD, Evermore)
- **Net effect:** the production is a quiet, wide-open bed — sparse drums, sustained pads, strings (Bryce Dessner orchestrations; London Contemporary Orchestra on TTPD) — that gives a modest, midrange, un-showy voice maximum presence and intelligibility. The voice is the loudest, driest, most human element in the mix; the formula inverts the pop default of burying the vocal in production.

## 6) Proof songs (2020–2025)

1. **"Anti-Hero" (Midnights, 2022)** — the self-loathing banger: 8 weeks at #1; the "rant bridge" nightmare (daughter-in-law murdering her for the inheritance — a literal third-section twist); hook sung flat ("It's me, hi / I'm the problem, it's me"); Antonoff: "so honest and funny, and also so sweet and so sad," built on "this weird beat going through a tremolo" with zero radio calculus. [Billboard, Oct 2023](https://www.billboard.com/music/pop/jack-antonoff-producer-tips-taylor-swift-bleachers-interview-1235435698/); [Wikipedia: Anti-Hero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-Hero_(song))
2. **"Cruel Summer" (2019 → 2023 re-chart)** — the four-year proof that her catalog compounds: never a single, became her 10th #1 off Eras Tour exposure; a textbook "rant bridge" ("He looks up, grinning like a devil") + "ooooh" hook; co-written with Antonoff + St. Vincent. Antonoff: "a huge thumbs-up from the universe… a reminder to do what you believe in." [Billboard, Oct 2023](https://www.billboard.com/music/pop/jack-antonoff-producer-tips-taylor-swift-bleachers-interview-1235435698/); [Wikipedia: Cruel Summer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cruel_Summer_(Taylor_Swift_song))
3. **"Fortnight" (TTPD, 2024)** — her 12th #1 (2 weeks), biggest single-day Spotify streams ever (Guinness); written with Post Malone + Antonoff; monotone verses, conversational hook, bridge/outro harmonies; Swift's stated craft: setting — "an American town where the American Dream never materialized." [Wikipedia: Fortnight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortnight_(song)); [Variety Hitmakers 2024](https://variety.com/2024/music/news/how-taylor-swift-and-jack-antonoff-keep-songwriting-fresh-1236235904/)
4. **"All Too Well (10 Minute Version)" (2021)** — the full pipeline demonstrated: 2011 improvised rant → recorded by a sound engineer → years of fan demand → rebuilt from memory/diaries with Liz Rose's discipline ("It literally just was that song, but it had probably seven extra verses… and it included the F-word" — RS 500 Greatest Albums podcast, via [Songfacts](https://www.songfacts.com/facts/taylor-swift/all-too-well)) → #1, longest song in Hot 100 history. The 10-minute version *adds* the humiliating details (the age-gap condescension, the missed 21st birthday, crying in a Hollywood bathroom) so the chorus's demand for respect "feels absolutely fully earned" (Vox). [Vox, Nov 2021](https://www.vox.com/culture/22778502/taylor-swift-all-too-well-10-minutes-red-rerelease-taylors-version)
5. **Supporting: "cardigan" (first act to debut #1 on Billboard 200 + Hot 100 same week), "willow" (#1, written in ~an hour over Dessner's "Westerly"), "Is It Over Now?" (#1 vault-track debut, 2023).**

## 8) "The trick" in their words — repeatable vs. one-off

- **Swift's own framing: the *process* is repeatable; the *inspiration* is not.** "They never quite happen exactly the same way, and I still don't quite understand how it works." (NYT 2026). The repeatable part: the idea-capture system (Notes-app word/title lists), the top-line ownership, the bridge-crescendo architecture, the callback structure, the concrete-noun details, the two co-writer lanes, and the late-album finishing sprint (Dessner's "she writes a lot of songs, and then at the very end she sometimes writes one or two more").
- **Dessner's formulation:** the "weird avalanche"/"lightning bolt" chemistry is situational, but the *method* — folders of instrumentals → fully written top lines in hours → voice memos — is a machine that produced ~30 songs across folklore + evermore in under a year, and was re-run for TTPD's Anthology (10 of 15 Anthology tracks co-written with Dessner).
- **Antonoff's formulation:** "I never know what's gonna happen next" — anti-formula as the formula; the constant is *consistency of environment* (same studio, same circle, no commercial self-consciousness): "no part of me was like, 'that's the song to take over the world'" — and it was.
- **Skeptical read:** the truly repeatable asset is the *volume + discipline of writing* (she wrote 2 albums + a re-recording during a pandemic year, then a 31-song double album mid-tour), the *detail-forward lyric doctrine* that makes every song a re-listen object, and *two interchangeable production lanes* so no single collaborator becomes a bottleneck. The one-off part is the cultural context (Eras Tour, master-recording narrative, dating headlines) that amplifies each release — none of which is a songwriting technique.
