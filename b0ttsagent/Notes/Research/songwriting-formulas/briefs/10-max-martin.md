## Summary
Max Martin (Karl Martin Sandberg, b. 1971) is the most documented "hit-every-time" methodologist in modern pop: he has written or co-written **30 Billboard Hot 100 #1 singles** (an all-time chart record as of June 2026), the **second-most #1s as a songwriter ever — behind only Paul McCartney (32)**, and won **ASCAP Songwriter of the Year a record 11 times** plus five Grammys. His repeatable framework — popularized as **"melodic math"** by John Seabrook in *The Song Machine* (Norton, 2015) and a 2015 *New Yorker* profile — is the closest thing modern pop has to a published formula, and it still produces 2020–2025 Hot 100 monuments via The Weeknd's "Blinding Lights," the **best-performing Hot 100 song of all time**.

## 2) The core repeatable workflow/formula — "Melodic Math"
The framework below is the synthesis John Seabrook published in *The Song Machine: Inside the Hit Factory* (W. W. Norton, 2015) and his *New Yorker* profile "Blank Space" (Oct 5, 2015), drawn from Seabrook's time inside Cheiron/Maratone and interviews with Martin's collaborators. **Max Martin almost never gives interviews**, so most of the granular methodology is **second-hand via Seabrook and co-producers (Shellback, Rami, Savan Kotecha, Oscar Holter)** — treat the specific numeric rules as Seabrook's distillation of the Cheiron/Maratone method, not as verbatim Max quotes. The verified direct quotes I do have are in §7.

The Cheiron/Maratone "track-first" method as Seabrook documents it:
1. **Track before topline.** The producer builds the instrumental track (beat, chords, bass, synth hooks) first; the vocal melody/topline is written *to* the track, not the other way around. This is the inversion of the singer-songwriter model and the core of "the song machine."
2. **Melodic math — a small number of melodic parts.** A Max Martin record is built from a deliberately limited set of melodic motifs (often described as ~three core parts: verse motif, pre-chorus lift, chorus). New parts are variations of existing ones, not brand-new melodies, so the brain recognizes them instantly. Repetition-with-variation is the engine.
3. **Chorus placement / "the chorus must arrive fast."** Seabrook reports the Cheiron discipline that the chorus (the hook) should hit early — commonly characterized as before the 60-second mark — so the listener gets the payoff before skipping. (Numeric framing is Seabrook's, not a verbatim Max quote.)
4. **"Monkey chant" / the idiot test.** Seabrook describes a Cheiron litmus test: if a hook can't be chanted by anyone — no words, just the melody and rhythm — it isn't strong enough. The topline must survive being sung as nonsense syllables; the melody *itself* is the hook, lyrics are secondary.
5. **New sound per section.** Each new section (verse → pre → chorus → post-chorus) is introduced by a new production element (a new synth, drum fill, vocal chop, riser) so the ear never gets bored even while the melody repeats. "Repetition with variation" at the arrangement level.
6. **The post-chorus.** Max Martin is widely credited with formalizing the **post-chorus** — a second hook section after the chorus that keeps the payoff going (Katy Perry "Roar," Taylor "Shake It Off," The Weeknd "Blinding Lights" all use it). It doubles hook exposure without wearing out the chorus.
7. **Lyrics serve the melody.** Because the topline is written to a finished track, words are chosen for vowel shapes, consonant punches, and rhythm first, meaning second. This is why Max's lyrics are famously ungrammatical (see §3) — syntax is sacrificed to melodic fit.
8. **Track-by-track, punch-in vocals.** Vocals are recorded in small sections, punched in line by line, comped from many takes. Not a "live performance" capture — an assembled, optimized vocal.

## 3) Lyric method
- **Lyrics are secondary to melody and rhythm** — written *after* the topline exists, shaped to vowel openings and consonant attacks that serve the hook. Slate's Forrest Wickman documented this in "The Most Puzzling, Ungrammatical Lyrics of Hitmaker Max Martin" (Slate, July 9, 2014), cataloging lines like Ariana Grande "Break Free" ("I only wanna die alive / Never by the hands of a broken heart"), Backstreet Boys "Shape of My Heart" ("Sadness is beautiful / loneliness is tragical"), Britney "(You Drive Me) Crazy" ("Tell me, I'm not in the blue"). Slate's thesis: the awkwardness is **a feature, not a bug** — the non-native-English Swedish writers prioritize phonetic fit and melodic rhyme over idiomatic grammar, which is exactly why the hooks stick. [Slate: The Most Puzzling, Ungrammatical Lyrics of Hitmaker Max Martin](https://slate.com/culture/2014/07/the-most-puzzling-ungrammatical-lyrics-of-hitmaker-max-martin.html)
- **One emotional thesis per song.** Max's hits distill to a single feeling/title conceit ("…Baby One More Time," "Roar," "Shake It Off," "Blinding Lights") and everything serves that one idea. The chorus *is* the thesis.
- **Co-writer-heavy.** Max almost never writes alone. "Blinding Lights" has five writers; "Shake It Off" has two (Max + Shellback + Swift + Ahmad); *Femme Fatale* tracks list Max + Dr. Luke + others. The room is the instrument.

## 4) Topline/singing method
- **The topline is the product.** The sung melody is composed to the finished track, then refined through many takes and comping. The Weeknd, Britney, Katy, Taylor are the *voices*, but the *melody* is Max's (co-)architecture.
- **Scale-laddering verses to a tension-filled chorus.** Slate's Chris Molanphy, on "Blinding Lights": *"From its scale-laddering verses to its tension-filled chorus, the song exhibits the polish and 'melodic math' for which Max Martin is renowned."* [Wikipedia: Blinding Lights, citing Slate/Molanphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blinding_Lights)
- **Falsetto/chest switches and "Hey! Hey! Hey!" chants** as rhythmic anchors (the "Blinding Lights" post-chorus chant is the monkey-chant test made literal).
- **Repetition-with-variation of the hook** across verse/pre/chorus/post-chorus so the same melody keeps returning in slightly new clothing.

## 5) Vocal-anchoring production
- The track is built *around and under* the topline as a counter-melody: "Blinding Lights" pairs Abel's vocal with a crystalline 1985-style synth riff that acts as a second hook (Variety called it "a crystalline synth hook straight out of 1985"). [Wikipedia: Blinding Lights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blinding_Lights)
- **New sound per section** (see §2) keeps the repeating melody from feeling stale.
- **Gated drums, synth bass, post-chorus drops** — the Cheiron/Maratone sonic signature updated to 2020s synthwave on *After Hours*.
- Mixer **Şerban Ghenea** (long-time Max collaborator) on "Blinding Lights": *"It was crossing two worlds to make something new that fits today, sonically. The older folks like it because it's a throwback, and then the kids love it because it's a new thing that they never heard before."* [Wikipedia: Blinding Lights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blinding_Lights)

## 8) "The trick" in their words
The repeatable engine is **melodic math**: a small set of melodic motifs, repeated-with-variation across verse → pre-chorus → chorus → **post-chorus**, with the chorus arriving fast and the topline written *to* a finished track so that vowel/consonant shape dictates the lyric. The hook must pass the "monkey chant" test — singable as nonsense, melody-first, lyrics-second. Max Martin's own on-the-record words are rare, but Britney's quote captures the practitioner experience: *"His melodies are incredible and he is always coming up with weird sounds."* The Weeknd's captures the working relationship: *"a collaboration is a relationship, it's like a marriage, you've gotta build up to it."* This is **the most repeatable formula in the entire 14-lane set** — by design, not accident. It has produced hits across three decades and four genres.
