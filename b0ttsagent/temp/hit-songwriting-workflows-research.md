# Verified Hitmakers' Songwriting Workflows — Lyric & Vocal Methods That Repeat

**What this is:** A deep, source-quoted breakdown of how verified, currently-active (2014–2025) chart-topping artists and their writer-producers actually make songs — focused on the **singing and lyric-writing** parts. Built from primary interviews (Rolling Stone, Billboard, GQ, Tape Op, Guardian, Variety, NME, Kerrang, Forbes, Grammys, etc.).

**Who's in:** Max Martin, Jack Antonoff + Taylor Swift, Finneas + Billie Eilish, Kendrick Lamar + Sounwave, SZA + her producers, Ryan Tedder, Sia, Benny Blanco, Olivia Rodrigo + Dan Nigro, Metro Boomin, Julia Michaels, Drake + Quentin Miller (the shadow-writer angle), Hozier, Imagine Dragons, plus briefer method-notes on Morgan Wallen, Zach Bryan, Twenty One Pilots, Sleep Token, and Lil Durk/Lil Baby.

**How to read each case:**
- **Credentials** = chart proof (fact, attributed).
- `> blockquotes` = the artist's own words (**evidence**, with a source tag like `[S2]`).
- *Interpretation:* = what I make of it (clearly labeled, not fact).
- *Takeaway:* = a recommendation you can act on.

## The recurring formula at a glance

Across very different genres, the people who do this *repeatedly* converge on the same handful of moves:

| # | Recurring move | Who does it (not exhaustive) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Hook/title/concept first** — write *toward* the payoff | Max Martin, Morgan Wallen, Taylor Swift, Sia, Imagine Dragons |
| 2 | **Melody-first**, phonetics and rhythm of words > literal meaning | Max Martin, Sia, Ryan Tedder, Imagine Dragons (classical piano) |
| 3 | **Recycle the melody across verse & chorus**, change the energy | Max Martin ("melodic math"), the backbone of modern pop |
| 4 | **Serve the artist's voice, not your signature** | Ryan Tedder, Finneas, Sia, Benny Blanco, Jack Antonoff |
| 5 | **Therapeutic intake** — extract the real feeling, then mine the phrasing | Julia Michaels, Benny Blanco, Sia, Olivia Rodrigo |
| 6 | **Song before production** — don't dress up a bad song | Finneas, Jack Antonoff |
| 7 | **Minimal verse → maximalist payoff** (sweet/salt contrast) | Max Martin, Finneas, Olivia/Dan Nigro |
| 8 | **Vulnerability over perfection** (often routed through characters) | Billie/Finneas, Taylor, Hozier, Olivia, SZA |
| 9 | **Obsessive high-volume output + ruthless filter** | Imagine Dragons (a song a day), Sia "productive not great", Wallen "zillion ideas, many terrible" |
| 10 | **Long-trust camp / the hit is a system, not a solo genius** | SZA's trio, Drake's OVO camp, Kendrick + Sounwave, Metro + 21 |

> **Caveat (read this before trusting any "formula"):** These are *workflows that produced hits*, not *guarantees of hits*.
> - **Survivorship bias:** we only study winners; thousands used the same methods and failed.
> - **Hits are increasingly co-written systems.** Most of these songs have 4–8 credited writers. A "solo genius" workflow is mostly a myth.
> - **Artists post-rationalize.** They may not reliably know *why* a song worked.
> - **Chart trends expire.** The "first chorus under 50 seconds" rule is already bending (see the 2024 data in the Limitations section).

## Part A — Deep case studies

### 1. Max Martin — the foundational "melodic math"

**Credentials:** Second only to Paul McCartney for the most #1 singles ever (beat John Lennon and George Martin's totals). Authored/co-authored era-defining hits from Britney's *"...Baby One More Time"* and Backstreet Boys' *"I Want It That Way"* through Katy Perry's *"Roar,"* Taylor Swift's *"Shake It Off"/"Blank Space,"* The Weeknd, Ariana Grande — still charting in 2024. The most-cited songwriter's songwriter. `[S1][S2][S3]`

> "You can also sing the chorus melody as a verse… The verse and chorus of that song are exactly the same. But as a listener, you don't really notice since the energy of the chorus is completely different compared to the verse. Once the chorus comes, you feel like you've heard it before. And you have!" — Max Martin, Polar Music Prize masterclass (2016) `[S2]`

> "To me, phonetics really matter. There's a big part of the world where [the listener] does not speak English. These songs are massive in those countries. My theory is about how it sounds and how it makes you feel when you hear these words." — Max Martin, to Tom Power (CBC *Q*, 2023) `[S2][S1]`

> "There shouldn't be too much information in the overall sound… One at a time. Like in a movie. You can't introduce ten characters in the first scene. You want to get to know one before you're ready for the next." — Max Martin, *Spin* (2017) `[S2]`

> "You must be able to have more than one favourite part in the same composition. First out, you might like the chorus. Then, once you've grown a little tired of that you must long for the bridge." `[S2]`

> "If you've got a verse with a lot of rhythm, you want to pair it with something that doesn't. Sweet and salt… You need a balance, at all times." `[S2]`

> "I like it when a song is like a journey, building up along the way… Then, at the end, euphoria." `[S2]`

Researcher Asaf Peres named the toolbox: the **"glue hook"** — "the lyrical counterpart of the melodic preview… He will use the *same lyric in different sections* to create a sense of familiarity and catchiness." And the **first-chorus-before-~50-seconds** rule, which Martin traces to Denniz Pop's club-DJ insight: if people don't recognize the next track in seconds, they leave the floor; "the whole thing was to keep them on the floor." `[S1]`

*Interpretation:* Martin's "melodic math" is misnamed — it's a *listener-psychology* system, not arithmetic. Four ideas do the heavy lifting: (1) write **one strong melody** and let it return in verse and chorus so the chorus feels "already known"; (2) make **how words sound** outrank what they literally mean (hence the odd grammar in his hits — "the one that I ever needed," "who I really am"); (3) introduce **one new element at a time**, like a film introducing characters; (4) engineer a **steady build to euphoria** with the chorus coming fast. Crucially, his "repetition" is *within a song* — he reuses the melody across sections — which is different from copying *across* songs (something Ryan Tedder actively avoids; see the Synthesis for the apparent contradiction).

*Takeaway:* Build the entire song on one motif you can stretch from verse to chorus; reintroduce it so its return feels like recognition, not novelty. Let the *sound* of the words drive the lyric, and never make the listener wait long for the payoff.

### 2. Jack Antonoff + Taylor Swift — long-trust collaboration & the "house" of an album

**Credentials:** Antonoff is a 3×-consecutive Grammy Producer of the Year (a feat tied only by Babyface); co-architect of Swift's *Midnights* (Album of the Year), *1989*, *Lover*, *folklore/evermore*, plus Lana Del Rey, Lorde, The 1975, and Kendrick. Swift is the most-awarded artist in Grammy history; over **100+ songs written together**. `[S4][S5]`

> "There's not a lot of panning back in the room — 'Whoa, look at this [achievement], look at that!' — because that would feel like popping the balloon… When I work with Taylor, there's still just this person who has these life experiences and this remarkable gift of writing about them." — Antonoff `[S4]`

> On "Anti-Hero": "no part of me was like, 'F–king A, that's the song to take over the world!'" `[S4]`

> "I've always believed in this 'house' mentality of just understanding what an album is, and 'Modern Girl' just feels like the biggest front door." `[S4]`

> "You never want to do anything that you don't believe in for the sake of success, because the only thing worse than doing something you don't believe in is being recognized for that thing!" — on "Cruel Summer" resurging to #3 years later `[S4]`

> With Lana: "We'll just text and be like, 'Where are you?' 'Okay, come over'… It's very loose." `[S6]`

On the *folklore* method (via Aaron Dessner, Swift's other key partner): "It doesn't seem like, for this record at least, that she was inspired to write something until she really knew what it was about… these narrative songs, often told from a different… not in the first person. So there are different characters in the songs that appear in others." `[S16]` Swift herself said her songs had "got too 'diaristic'" and the folklore/evermore turn was into **character and myth**. `[S17]` Dessner: "We really wanted to keep her voice as human, and kind of the opposite of plastic, as possible." `[S16]`

*Interpretation:* Antonoff's workflow is anti-pressure. He deliberately keeps commercial stakes *out of the room* (so the song isn't flinched into safe shapes), works in **long-trust pairs** (100+ songs with Swift = a shared vocabulary that removes friction), and treats an album as a **"house"** — each song a room; the lead single the "front door" that sets expectations. Swift's lyric method pivots between two modes: the razor-diaristic (*Midnights*) and the character-mask (*folklore*) — same emotional truth, different distance. The constant is a **plastic-free, human vocal**.

*Takeaway:* Separate the writing room from the chart conversation; cultivate one or two long-term partners where the "getting to know you" tax is already paid; and decide per-song whether to write *as yourself* or *as a character* — both can carry real feeling, but the character lens lets you write about things you'd otherwise flinch from.

### 3. Finneas + Billie Eilish — minimalism, space, and the un-tuned voice

**Credentials:** Youngest-ever Grammy Producer of the Year (Non-Classical); 10 Grammys, 2 Oscars. Co-wrote and produced *When We All Fall Asleep…*, *Happier Than Ever*, *Hit Me Hard and Soft* (2024) — multiple Hot 100 #1s. `[S7][S8][S9]`

> "The song comes first… if I can write a bad song *before* devoting hours of production time into my bad idea, that is smarter." `[S7]`

> "I wanted Billie's voice to have this three-dimensionality in a song — something you could see if you closed your eyes. The only way I could do that was to carve all of this space out, so the less stuff that was I putting in there, the better." `[S7]`

> "Billie has such great pitch… Auto-Tune doesn't rock with [glissando] super easily… what I'm least moved by is some pop artist singing over drums and keys where everything is quantized. That's the music that bums me out. I'm not usually bummed out by an emotional vocal performance that gets a little pitchy. That usually delights me!" `[S7]`

> "I play all of the shakers and tambourines, and sometimes my tambourine playing is pretty bad. But it's cooler to me to have a whacky tambourine than an amazing loop that I pulled from some library." `[S7]`

> "What I try to do is watch the other person start to run, and then I try to keep pace… it feels more like the authentic work of the artist that I'm just the guest of." (Plus the classic co-write move: "'Well, not this, but maybe something *kind of* like this?'") `[S7]`

He records Foley on an iPhone (a stereo hack: take video, strip the audio), keeps vocals **dry and close** (Valhalla Room/VintageVerb only when needed), tracked an Oscar-winning Bond vocal on a tour-bus bunk. `[S7]`

*Interpretation:* Finneas's method is the inverse of Max Martin's density-as-journey — it's **subtraction as intimacy**. The "song before production" rule is the spine: get the lyric and melody undeniable *on a single instrument* before adding anything, because polishing a bad idea with $20k of gear still leaves a bad idea. Refusing to Auto-Tune Billie preserves her glissando and therefore her emotion; the imperfections are the signal, not noise. And he produces by **listening for the artist's direction and keeping pace** rather than imposing his own.

*Takeaway:* Strip the track until the vocal has "oxygen." Lock the song on one instrument before you touch a plugin. Resist tuning what should feel human, and prefer a flawed real performance over a perfect sampled loop. When producing others, wait for them to "point the needle" and then support — don't lead.

### 4. Kendrick Lamar + Sounwave — the car-ride test, piano-as-world, no yes-men

**Credentials:** Pulitzer Prize winner; 2024 back-to-back Hot 100 #1s with *"Not Like Us"* and *"Squabble Up"*; *GNX* dropped late 2024. Producer Sounwave has been his core collaborator since *good kid*. `[S10][S11][S12][S38]`

> "I remember about a week before the album was coming out… I looked at Kendrick and I said, 'You know it's not going to come out unless we take this car ride.' And he was like 'Yup.'… I *have* to feel this overwhelming feeling of 'Wow, this is it. It's completed.' And if I don't have that, I'm going to immediately call him up and say, 'It's not ready.'" — Sounwave `[S10]`

> "98 percent of the song is going to have a piano in there… a person stripped down of everything, locked into a white room with just their thoughts and a piano." `[S10]`

> "If I'm listening to a song and I find myself drifting at a certain moment, I'm like, 'Okay, that's the part where I have to do something completely different right here.'" `[S10]`

> On "We Cry Together": Kendrick "had pitched his voice up to sound like a girl and he was literally arguing with himself." `[S10]`

> "There's no yes-men in our circle." `[S10]`

The process is long and migratory: early *Mr. Morale* brainstorming happened in London ("only one song made it" — *"Father Time"*); the bulk came only after a pandemic reset and a Joshua Tree trip Sounwave credits with "unlocking everything." `[S10]`

*Interpretation:* Kendrick's system is built on **friction + honesty, not speed**. The "car-ride finish test" is a forced gut-check — if it doesn't make him *feel* finished, it's not done, regardless of deadlines. The piano-as-spine choice makes the record feel confessional (where *TPAB* was maximalist). The "I'm drifting → fix it now" rule is a literal **listener-attention restoration** loop. And "arguing with himself" by pitching his own voice is character-writing applied to a duet — emotional truth delivered through performance, not autobiography.

*Takeaway:* Adopt a non-negotiable finish-test you can't cheat (the car ride). Pick one sound as the album's emotional spine and manipulate it enough that it never gets boring. When you notice your own attention dip, that's the cut point — insert a change. Keep at least one person in the room whose job is to say "this doesn't need to be there."

### 5. SZA + her producers (Lang, Bisel, ThankGod4Cody) — camp-out trust, "write the music you'd want to hear"

**Credentials:** *SOS* spent **10 non-consecutive weeks at #1** on the Billboard 200; *"Kill Bill"* was her first Hot 100 #1; follow-up *Lana* (2024). The trio has credits on 19 of *SOS*' 23 songs. `[S13][S14][S15]`

> Carter Lang: "We'd all bunker up and pretty much camp out in the stu' and just be making stuff for weeks, if not months, at a time. Those adventures bonded us for life." `[S14]`

> Lang: "She takes her time to get in her zone, so it's about being patient with each other… it feels more like vibing out around a campfire." `[S14]`

> "Kill Bill" — Rob Bisel: "She was writing down lyrics. It's kind of silent for five to 10 minutes. And then she just says to me, 'I have an idea. This might be a little too crazy, but let me know what you think.' And I was just like, 'You just did it again.' It was instantly undeniable." `[S13]`

> Bisel: "She'd have songs like 'I Hate U' or 'Kill Bill' where she would write them in under an hour. The ideas flowed more effortlessly… her pen got sharper and more personal." `[S14]`

> Bisel: "Make the music that you would want to listen to yourself." (on "Kill Bill") `[S14]`

> ThankGod4Cody: "She's writing all of these songs and they come from such a genuine place. That is the glue." — and on producing: "it's about you setting the vibe of the whole room… making sure that the artist is good and comfortable and in the best space to get out whatever ideas they have." `[S14]`

*Interpretation:* SZA's team treats the studio as **long-form hangout**, not the clock-on-the-wall hit factory. Speed (an hour for "Kill Bill") emerges *from comfort*, not from pressure — the years of camp-out trust made the fast late-stage writing possible. The producer's real job, per Cody, is **vibe-management**: the room has to feel safe enough that the artist blurts the "too crazy" idea. And "make what you'd want to hear yourself" is the *quality filter* replacing external trend-chasing.

*Takeaway:* Optimize the room for psychological safety and patience, not output — the undeniable ideas surface when no one's rushing.writer When something feels "too crazy," that's a signal to chase it, not censor it. Filter every song by "would I actually want to hear this?" not "will it chart?"

### 6. Ryan Tedder — the anti-signature, channel-the-artist rulebook

**Credentials:** Writer/producer of Beyoncé's *"Halo,"* Adele's *"Rumour Has It,"* Leona Lewis' *"Bleeding Love,"* Ellie Goulding's *"Burn,"* Jonas Brothers' *"Sucker"* + OneRepublic's own *"Counting Stars."* A top-tier songwriter for ~15 years. `[S18][S19]`

> "The night before I work with an artist, I read about them, I channel them. I'm desperately trying *not* to give them my sound… Nothing is less cool than sounding like last summer's dance hit." `[S18]`

> "Always match a song with the right singer… A couple of times, I've yanked songs from artists because, no matter how hard we tried, we couldn't get it right. If it's not a 10, then it's not worth doing." `[S18]`

> "People react to humanity in songs." `[S18]`

> "I always try to find the clever way to say something — ultimately, you're talking about love in different forms… You need that turn of phrase." — citing "the Nashville school of writing… desperately seeking *the* phrase — one that I can relate to and that people in general use, but don't realise [the power of]." `[S18]`

> "Anyone who has been in a session with me would describe it as barely controlled chaos… I'm genuinely chasing goosebumps." `[S18]`

> "Try not to repeat yourself… If I sense that I'm doing something too often, I'll back away." `[S18]`

**Credentials note:** He's older than the "last 5 years" window for his hottest solo placements, but OneRepublic is still charting (*Artificial Paradise*, 2024) and his *method* is aggressively cited. `[S18]`

*Interpretation:* Tedder's whole method is the **opposite of a signature sound** — he believes a producer's footprint becomes a sell-by date, so he pre-studies each artist and tries to disappear into them. His lyric move is the Nashville **"turn of phrase" hunt**: find a phrase people *use but don't notice*, and load it until it carries the song. The "if it's not a 10, yank it" rule is brutal quality control. And "chasing goosebumps" is his word for the somatic test — the body knows before the brain.

*Takeaway:* Before a session, research the artist like you're preparing to *imitate* them, then consciously suppress your own sonic habits. Spend disproportionate effort hunting one *phrase* that reframes a familiar feeling ("I don't want a parachute"). Trust the goosebumps as the decision signal; if it isn't a 10, cut it.

### 7. Sia — melody-first, concept-list-on-phone, "check your ego and become their bitch"

**Credentials:** Wrote Rihanna's *"Diamonds"* (≈12–15 minutes), David Guetta's *"Titanium,"* Beyoncé's *"Pretty Hurts,"* Katy Perry cuts, Adele's *"Alive"*, plus her own *"Chandelier."* `[S20][S21][S22]`

> "I probably get 20 or 30 tracks a week from my producer friends who are hoping that I'll write lyrics and melody on top of them. I write over them in my house… If I know Rihanna is looking for a single, I'll actually choose tracks that sound like a Rihanna-like jam." `[S20]`

> "Melody comes first, and then I'll choose lyrical content from a list of concepts I have in my phone, and whenever I think of one, I write it down… I might have something called 'Bubble Gum,' and I'll think, 'Yeah, that's not Rihanna, that's more for a younger artist.'" `[S20]`

> "I think the reason I'm pretty successful is actually because I'm really productive, not necessarily that I'm a great songwriter. I think I'm a good curator… [the public] responds well to songs about salvation or overcoming something, or that everything's going to be OK, or that things are fun." `[S20]`

> "I basically check my ego at the door and I become their bitch… Words come out and it's just like, 'bluh.'… I don't overthink it. If a word or sentence sounds silly, then I'll change it, but I'm not analytical." `[S20]`

> "You're basically throwing spaghetti at a wall and hoping it will stick. You start with the people you really can hear… Then if they reject it, you start thinking more creatively or broadly." `[S20]`

Songfacts documents her *"Diamonds"* breakthrough method: "start with a basic image and use it as a metaphor for empowerment, supporting that single theme with the lyrics." `[S22]`

*Interpretation:* Sia's workflow is the most *industrialized* in this doc, and she's candid that **productivity > talent**. She keeps a **rolling concept list on her phone**, hears a track, instantly matches it to a likely recipient artist, writes melody-first, then bolts on a concept from the list — almost always a **salvation/overcoming** empowerment metaphor (one image → the whole lyric theme). She drafts fast and non-analytically ("all channels"), then repurposes rejects down a ranked list. The ego-as-service framing is the producer-ethic extreme.

*Takeaway:* Keep a permanent, growing concept/title list on your phone — capture fragments the moment they occur. When a backing track arrives, match it to a *specific* intended artist before you write a note. Default to empowerment/overcoming themes; anchor each song on a single visual metaphor. Write fast and uncensored, then curate.

### 8. Benny Blanco — home studio, capture accidents, be the therapist

**Credentials:** Baker's dozen #1s incl. "Teenage Dream," "Moves Like Jagger," "TiK ToK," "Stereo Hearts"; co-writer/producer for Justin Bieber, Ed Sheeran, Halsey, The Weeknd. `[S23]`

> "My studio's always in my house. I want to wake up and be like, 'You know I'm gonna make music today in my underwear.' … When I'm in a big studio, I get anxious." `[S23]`

> "Sometimes we start a song with a melody, sometimes we start it with a guitar part, sometimes we're playing ping pong and the ball will fall in a certain way and we're like 'Oh my God we have to record that.' There's no rhyme or reason… the creativity, it always hits you at the most awkward times." `[S23]`

Fast Company frames his role explicitly as **"balancing the role of creative partner and therapist"** and "knowing when to play ping-pong." `[S23]`

*Interpretation:* Blanco's method is deliberately **unstructured-by-design** to lower the stakes and raise the accident rate. Home studio = no clock-driven anxiety; "no rhyme or reason" start points = the muse is treated as random, so you just need to *be available* when it strikes (the ping-pong ball). And the "therapist" framing is the same intake move as Julia Michaels (next) — get the artist talking about their real life, then songify it.

*Takeaway:* Remove the pressure of "studio time" by making the writing space somewhere frictionless and personal. Abandon any dogma about where a song has to *start*; carry a recorder and honor the bizarre origin moments. Treat the first 20 minutes with an artist as a conversation, not a session.

### 9. Olivia Rodrigo + Dan Nigro — lyric-focus + structure-focus split, minimal→maximalist bridge

**Credentials:** *"Drivers License"* and *"Good 4 U"* both debuted at #1 (first debut album in history to spawn two #1-debuting singles); *SOUR* biggest sales week of 2021; *GUTS* (2023). Nigro produced the entire album and shares writing on 8 of 11 *SOUR* tracks. `[S24][S25]`

> Dan Nigro: "I think the balance lies in the fact that Olivia is so lyric-focused, while I'm more melody- and texture-focused. Her main objective while we're working on each song is making sure that every word hits exactly how it needs to, while I obsess over each chord and which inversion of it to play." `[S24]`

> Nigro: "I do tend to want a little more structure melodically, so I'll interject here or there when I feel like there are too many words in a stanza or too little… and then we'll symmetrically [or] melodically work it out." `[S24]`

> On "Drivers License": "The verse and chorus are super minimal, which is what Olivia was gravitating towards at that moment and the bridge was maximalist, which was something I was more into." `[S24]`

> The "Brutal" opener "I want it to be, like, messy" was recorded by accident and sampled into the intro. `[S24]`

> NPR: before *GUTS*, she "first had to write a lot of 'bad songs.'" `[S25]`

*Interpretation:* Their partnership is an explicit **division of labor**: the lyricist guards every word; the producer guards structure and texture. The recurring sonic move is the **minimal-verse / maximalist-bridge contrast** — letting the bridge do the emotional detonation the verses withhold. And they treat "mistakes" (a frustrated "I want it to be messy," a seatbelt-beep morph) as material.

*Takeaway:* Split the lyric and the structure between two obsessives — one who'd die on every word, one who'd die on every chord. Plan your dynamic around a *withheld* verse and a *released* bridge. Record everything, including your exasperated reactions — the throwaway line may be the intro.

### 10. Metro Boomin — skeleton-then-iterate, the "real producer" coaches

**Credentials:** 115 Hot 100 songs produced, incl. 10 top-10 and 2 #1s (Migos' "Bad and Boujee," Weeknd's "Heartless"); *Heroes & Villains* #1 album (2022); executive-produced the *Spider-Verse* soundtracks. `[S26][S27]`

> "I'm a producer who doesn't rap, so I have to speak through like the production of it. A lot of [my beats] will just be like skeletons of stuff… ideas, just to get an idea going. Then an artist might do some ideas on it and I'll go back in and f**k with it some more. Have another producer come back and f**k with it some more then have the artist come back in." `[S26]`

> "the kids are always gonna sniff out the good s**t. Always… if you just focus on that and bringing the s**t… versus you tryna like, cater, kinda like fake." (citing Pharrell) `[S26]`

> "I've grown from more of a beatmaker role to more of a real producer role… I might give you some bars, I might help you start this off, I might let you know like yo, this was hard, but you started to lose me halfway through. Let's just fix that up… I can't just sit there in the studio and you're just rapping on it and I might not like it and I'm just going with it." `[S26]`

> On Future: "Future treats our ears like they're his… We'll mix a whole album and he won't even hear it but he knows we'll have it in the right place." `[S26]`

Billboard: his signature is **eerie synth loops, 808s, soulful samples and orchestral finishes**, branded by producer tags, and curated as a **"cinematic universe"** of recurring collaborators (21 Savage, Future, Travis Scott, Don Toliver — whom he cast as "the Nate Dogg of Heroes & Villains"). `[S27]`

*Interpretation:* Metro's trap workflow is **iterative and skeletal**: ship a beat-skeleton the artist can react to, then ping-pong between producer and artist until it's a record. Crucially his "real producer" definition expands past making beats to **coaching the vocal** — suggesting bars, flagging where he "started to lose me." And he's genre-defining not by chasing virality but by building a **recognizable, recurring world** with a stable cast (the hip-hop analogue of Taylor's "house").

*Takeaway:* Don't finalize a beat in isolation; ship skeletons that invite a vocal, then iterate in rounds. Treat the vocal as your responsibility too — give bars, mark the drift. Build a recognizable recurring cast/sound so your work becomes a *world* listeners opt into, not a playlist of one-offs.

### 11. Julia Michaels — the "musical therapist," phrase-mining the vent

**Credentials:** Writer of Justin Bieber's *"Sorry,"* Selena Gomez's *"Lose You to Love Me"* (#1), Hailee Steinfeld's *"Love Myself,"* Britney cuts; Grammy-nominated artist in her own right. `[S28]`

> "I'm like, 'Come in. Sit on the chair. Let's talk. What's going on? What happened this week? Did that make you upset?' … I take everything they said in a span of 20 minutes and help them piece it together. I always try to get the most out of people." `[S28]`

> "When they tell me what they're going through, my ear always goes to the thing that I can connect to… in their venting, there will be a word or a phrase they'll say — for example, Gomez's cheeky 'Hands to Myself' refrain — and you're like, 'Oh my God! That's an amazing song!'" `[S28]`

> On "Sorry" and Bieber: "I knew that he was a very emotional person, just from the way he picks songs and writes songs… People are afraid to say, 'Yeah, I'm fucking upset. I miss her.'" `[S28]`

*Interpretation:* Michaels' method is the **intake interview** — 20 minutes of conversation as the raw material, then *listen for the stray phrase* that escaped the artist's own notice ("Hands to Myself" came from a venting line). She explicitly plays the non-judgmental friend and leans into the demographic she's strongest with — emotionally-guarded men — cracking the armor is the song.

*Takeaway:* Open every co-write with an unhurried "how was your week." Don't transcribe the story; transcribe the **offhand phrases**, because the hook is usually the thing the artist said without meaning to. Normalize the ugly feeling ("I'm upset, I miss her") — permission to say it is permission to write it.

### 12. Drake + Quentin Miller — the shadow-writer / reference-track system

**Credentials:** Drake — most charted Hot 100 entries by a soloist; 2024–2025 (the Kendrick feud) kept him omnipresent. The Quentin Miller episode (2015) is the cleanest documented case of the modern **co-writer / reference-track** model in rap. `[S29][S30]`

> Quentin Miller (Tumblr): "I am not and never will be a 'ghostwriter' for drake" — he was a **credited co-writer** in the *If You're Reading This It's Too Late* liner notes (5 songs), not an uncredited ghost. `[S29]`

> Drake (to DJ Semtex): "Quentin Miller is a kid I was introduced to through Boi-1da. Me and Boi-1da were working on a project at the time. I said to him, 'I want to do a mixtape.'" `[S30]`

The mechanic, per the Complex liner-notes analysis: a writer cuts a **full "reference track"** — lyrics, flow, cadence, sometimes a whole performance — and the headline artist adapts it to their own voice. Co-writing credits are filed. `[S29]`

*Interpretation:* This is the "shadow writer has something important" angle you flagged, and it reveals that **consistent rap output is increasingly a camp system, not a solo pen**. The workflow that *reliably ships* is: a rotating bench of reference writers (Miller, Boi-1da's circle), a house production/engineering spine (Noah "40" Shebib), and the star as the **final filter and voice** — adapting bounce, flow, and charisma. The "writer" skill that matters here is *flow-and-cadence drafting*, not finishing; the "drake-ness" is the adaptation layer. (It's the rap equivalent of Sia cutting a demo Bey/Rihanna then re-homing it.)

*Takeaway:* If you want *consistent output*, build a bench: producers, reference writers, an engineer who defines the sonic brand — and treat yourself as the **curator/adaptor**, not the sole originator. Draft flow and cadence first (even via reference recordings); the final "you" is in the adaptation, not the blank page.

### 13. Hozier — the "does it move me" test, character-distance, literary frameworks

**Credentials:** *"Too Sweet"* hit **#1 on the Hot 100** in 2024 (his highest since *"Take Me to Church,"* 2014); *Unreal Unearth* (2023) + *Unheard/Unaired* EPs. Doubly-platinum. `[S39]`

> "Does it move me or not? Is it something that I feel is urgent to me, and it feels worth writing? If ever the answer is no… 'Let somebody else do it.'" `[S39]`

> "What seemed like an intolerable path to tread after 'Church'… was, like, I don't want to change my shape, my intentions, and my approach to the work — and to myself — based on what is perceived to be a charting hit." `[S39]`

> He doesn't want songs to feel like "a journal entry, or a work that trades off on some intrigue into my private life" — so he often tells stories through **fictional characters** for distance. `[S39]`

> Using Dante's *Inferno* as the *Unreal Unearth* framework: "Every chapter is a different character voicing their pain, their suffering, whatever it is. They're speaking to him about the life that they lived." `[S39]`

> A spontaneous jam with Bekon (Kendrick's producer): "It was unburdened with too much consideration. Unburdened from the need for this to be *the thing*." `[S39]`

Notably, *"Too Sweet"* — his biggest hit in a decade — was **left off** the album ("'People don't want to hear this stuff, we'll just put it away'") and only came out on a vault EP; its opening lyrics were partly inspired by a real anecdote (bleeding on a plane next to Cillian Murphy). `[S39]`

*Interpretation:* Hozier's method is **integrity-as-filter + distance-as-craft**. The single rule ("does it move me?") is a quality gate that lets him discard chart logic. He routes autobiography *through characters and through a literary frame* (Dante) — same emotional access, none of the journal-entry exposure. And he holds the reframe that a song you almost shelved can be the one that goes viral.

*Takeaway:* Use a single somatic gate ("does this move *me*?") and let it overrule commercial reasoning. When material is too personal, give it to a character or hang it on a literary scaffold — distance unlocks honesty you can't access in first person. Don't trust your own shelving instinct; the track you nearly buried may be the single.

### 14. Imagine Dragons / Dan Reynolds — song-a-day volume, stream-of-consciousness, classical-piano melody

**Credentials:** Four RIAA **diamond** singles ("Radioactive," "Believer," "Demons," "Thunder" — each 1B+ streams); ~75M albums; one of the few rock acts with sustained streaming-era dominance; *Loom* (2024). `[S40][S41]`

> "I started writing when I was 12, and I have recorded a song almost every day since then. I have thousands and thousands of recorded songs with lyrics and melody that never saw the light of day." `[S41]`

> "What I learned is just not to overthink. I always get on the computer and put a microphone in front of me and just whatever the output is that day is just what it is… move through it as quickly as possible." `[S40]`

> "It often starts with me just saying whatever is being felt with that rhythm or melodic soundscape… stream-of-consciousness creation. And then I'll pull out a notepad and a pen and start writing actual lyrics." — e.g., "Kid" began with a mumbled "You've got to get yourself together, kid." `[S40]`

> "I don't typically like writing lyrics or melody in front of anybody. But [Mattman & Robin] feel like family to me." `[S40]`

> On "Whatever It Takes": "We kept feeling like the chorus sounded too familiar… too close to Gary Glitter's 'Rock 'n' Roll.' We got rid of the chorus. I rewrote and re-recorded the chorus." `[S41]`

> His melodic sensibility comes from **classical piano** ("Beethoven, Bach, Chopin… a lot of thirds and fifths and harmonies") plus 70s singer-songwriters (Paul Simon, Billy Joel, Cat Stevens, Dylan). `[S40]`

Notably, for *Loom* (2024) they **scrapped ~150 demos and wrote fresh in-room** — the opposite of their usual demo-pick process. `[S40]`

*Interpretation:* Reynolds' method is **volume as the engine, stream-of-consciousness as the ignition, self-edit as the brake**. The daily-song discipline isn't about quality-per-song — it's about *keeping the channel open* so the inevitable 10/100 surfaces. Stream-of-consciousness mumbling over a beat gets the subconscious to lead (same instinct as Sia's "all channels"). The rewrite of a too-familiar chorus is the **inverse-earworm discipline** — actively killing anything that reads as derivative. The classical-piano root is why his choruses *climb* like piano etudes.

*Takeaway:* Treat a daily song as hygiene, not masterpieces — keep the tap flowing so luck can strike. Start from mumbled stream-of-consciousness over a groove; let the actual *words* fall out of feeling first, refine second. Actively police for "sounds too much like ____," and rewrite the offending section.

## Part B — Briefer method-notes (broad coverage)

- **Morgan Wallen** (2024–2025, *"I Had Some Help"* #1, *I'm the Problem*): **concept + payoff hook first, write toward it.** "We won't even start a song unless we have a full concept and the payoff hook done at the beginning. Then we'll start the first verse to write toward that hook." Exception ("Genesis") proved the rule by going backward. "I've got a zillion ideas in my brain — a lot of them are terrible." `[S31][S32]` *Takeaway:* lock the title and the hook landing-line before verse one.

- **Zach Bryan** (current, *The Great American Bar Scene*): raw, unfiltered, self-released; Nebraska/Springsteen/Bruce lineage ("Deliver me from nowhere" tattoo); stripped acoustic narrative. `[S33]` *Takeaway:* the workflow is unmediated confession — write it, record it, release it; the polish *is* the anti-polish.

- **Twenty One Pilots / Tyler Joseph** (*Clancy*, 2024): builds a **decade-long concept-world** (Dema → Trench → Clancy) across albums; genre-blends emo/rap/electronica; competitive-benchmarks classics ("Why can't I write a song that good?" re: Oasis' "Wonderwall"). `[S34][S35]` *Takeaway:* a recurring fictional universe across releases is a retention engine.

- **Sleep Token / Vessel** (*Take Me Back to Eden*, 2023; *Even in Arcadia*, 2025): **anonymous lore + masked members + a trilogy arc**; cross-genre (metal + pop + R&B + jazz); core writing duo "Vessel + II." "Our identity is represented through the art itself." `[S36]` *Takeaway:* anonymity + lore + genre-meld = a cult before a single; the saga is the product.

- **Lil Durk & Lil Baby** ("melodic rap"): Lil Durk's self-named **"Melodize Drill"** — "I be singing a little bit behind the verses, ad-libs… even if I rap hard, you always gon hear melodize somewhere. I think that's what makes me different." Lil Baby cites Gunna's melodic-flow influence. `[S37]` *Takeaway:* in rap hooks, sing *behind* and *around* the verses (ad-lib melodies), not just in the chorus.

## Part D — Patterns that recur across legends (the synthesis)

Below, *evidence* = direct quoted moves; *interpretation* = my read; the patterns are ordered by how strongly they recur.

### D1. Anchor the song before you write a verse — hook, title, or concept first.
*Evidence:* Wallen ("won't start… without a full concept and the payoff hook done" `[S31]`); Swift via Dessner ("not inspired to write something until she really knew what it was about" `[S16]`); Sia (chooses a concept from her phone-list before lyrics `[S20]`); Max Martin (the whole song engineered around the strongest hook `[S2]`).
*Interpretation:* The title/hook is the *target*; verses are missiles. Writing forward into a void is far less reliable than writing *toward* a known landing line. This is the single most consistent rule across pop, R&B, country, and rock alike.

### D2. Melody first; let how the words *sound* outrank what they *mean*.
*Evidence:* Max Martin ("phonetics really matter… my theory is about how it sounds and how it makes you feel" `[S2]`); Sia ("Melody comes first" `[S20]`); Tedder ("copy the Scandinavians' use of melody" `[S18]`); Imagine Dragons (classical-piano melodic sensibility drives the choruses `[S40]`).
*Interpretation:* A lyric can be grammatically broken and still iconic if the *phonetics* lock into the melody ("the one that I ever needed"). Prioritize the vowel shapes, consonant attacks, and rhythm of the words over literal sense — you can fix meaning later; you can't fix a clunky melodic setting.

### D3. Recycle one melody across sections; introduce one new element at a time.
*Evidence:* Max Martin's "verse and chorus are exactly the same… you don't really notice since the energy of the chorus is completely different" `[S2]`, and "one at a time. Like in a movie" `[S2]`.
*Interpretation:* Familiarity = memorability. Returning the same motif in a denser "energy" makes the chorus feel inevitable. Layering one new element at a time keeps the listener's cognitive load low enough to keep grabbing them.

### D4. Serve the artist's voice — kill your signature.
*Evidence:* Tedder ("desperately trying *not* to give them my sound" `[S18]`); Finneas ("watch the other person start to run, and then I try to keep pace" `[S7]`); Sia ("I basically check my ego at the door and become their bitch" `[S20]`); Antonoff (keeps the artist's superstardom *outside* the room `[S4]`).
*Interpretation:* The producer's job is to disappear into the artist's vernacular. A signature sound has a sell-by date; an artist's voice doesn't.

### D5. Intake before writing — get the real feeling, then mine the phrasing.
*Evidence:* Michaels ("Come in. Sit on the chair… what happened this week?… take everything they said in 20 minutes" `[S28]`); Blanco (therapist role `[S23]`); Sia ("become their bitch" `[S20]`).
*Interpretation:* The song's emotional fuel comes from *conversation*, not blank-page lyricism. The hook is almost always a phrase the artist said *without intending to* — your job is to be listening for it.

### D6. Song before production; don't dress up a bad idea.
*Evidence:* Finneas ("if I can write a bad song before devoting hours of production time… that is smarter" `[S7]`); Antonoff (the song, not the polish, is the point `[S4]`).
*Interpretation:* A polished turd is still a turd. Make the song undeniable on a single instrument before adding a single plugin.

### D7. Minimal verse → maximalist payoff (sweet/salt).
*Evidence:* Max Martin ("sweet and salt… you need a balance" `[S2]`); Finneas (carve space for the vocal `[S7]`); Olivia/Dan Nigro (minimal verse, maximalist bridge `[S24]`).
*Interpretation:* Dynamic contrast does the emotional work. Withhold in the verse to *release* in the chorus/bridge.

### D8. Vulnerability over perfection — often routed through characters.
*Evidence:* Finneas (refuses to tune Billie; "an emotional vocal performance that gets a little pitchy… delights me" `[S7]`); Hozier ("does it move me?" `[S39]`); Taylor (character/folklore lens over diary `[S16][S17]`).
*Interpretation:* Authenticity ≠ autobiography. You can deliver raw feeling *through a character* and keep your privacy intact. Imperfections in the vocal are *evidence* of real feeling — don't scrub them out.

### D9. Obsessive volume + ruthless filter.
*Evidence:* Imagine Dragons ("a song almost every day… thousands that never saw light" `[S41]`); Sia ("I'm really productive, not necessarily a great songwriter… a good curator" `[S20]`); Wallen ("a zillion ideas… a lot terrible" `[S31]`).
*Interpretation:* Reliability is a numbers game dressed as talent. The lottery-by-output system: produce far more than you'll release, then curate hard.

### D10. The hit is a *camp*, not a solo genius.
*Evidence:* SZA's 19/23-song trio `[S14]`; Drake's OVO bench + reference-writer system `[S29][S30]`; Kendrick + Sounwave's closed camp `[S10]`; Metro's recurring cast "cinematic universe" `[S27]`; Swift's decade-long Antonoff/Dessner pairs `[S4][S16]`.
*Interpretation:* Consistency is structural, not individual. The artists who "make a famous song every time" are really **systems** — a trusted bench of writers, producers, and an engineer who defines the sonic brand. The star is the filter and the voice, rarely the sole originator.

### D11. The "make what you'd want to hear" test over trend-chasing.
*Evidence:* SZA/Bisel ("Make the music that you would want to listen to yourself" `[S14]`); Hozier ("does it move me?" `[S39]`); Metro ("the kids sniff out the good s\*\*t… don't fake-cater" `[S26]`); Antonoff ("don't do anything you don't believe in" `[S4]`).
*Interpretation:* The most reliable quality filter is *self* — would I genuinely want to hear this? Trend-chasing has the shortest shelf life; self-approval + high output has the longest.

### D12. World-building / re-occurring continuity as a retention engine.
*Evidence:* Twenty One Pilots (Dema/Trench/Clancy saga `[S34][S35]`); Sleep Token (anonymous trilogy `[S36]`); Hozier (Dante framework `[S39]`); Metro (heroes/villains universe `[S27]`); Swift (cross-album character cameos in folklore/evermore `[S16]`).
*Interpretation:* A recurring fictional universe converts one-off listeners into *citizens* of a world. Lore is a retention mechanism that survives any single song's chart life.

## Part E — Where the "rules" disagree (read these carefully)

These apparent contradictions matter — resolving them tells you *when* a rule applies.

- **"Don't repeat yourself" (Tedder `[S18]`) vs. "Reuse the same melody verse & chorus" (Max Martin `[S2]`).** *Resolution:* Tedder means don't repeat *across your own songs*; Max Martin means reuse *within one song*. Both are right — burn your motif out inside one track; never photocopy a past track.
- **"Get to the chorus fast / under ~50s" (Max Martin, club-DJ origin `[S1]`) vs. 2024 chart data (first chorus averaging **45s**, a quarter after the 1-minute mark `[S38]`).** *Resolution:* the *principle* (don't make them wait too long for the payoff) is intact, but the *threshold* has shifted with streaming + the rise of hip-hop/country #1s. In 2024, **69% of #1s had three choruses** (up from 31% in 2022) and songs got *longer* (avg 3:30) `[S38]` — so the trend is "more choruses, but arrive a little later."
- **"Raw vulnerability / humanity" (Tedder, Finneas `[S18][S7]`) vs. "play-acting / character-masks" (Sia, Swift folklore, Hozier `[S20][S16][S39]`).** *Resolution:* emotional truth can be *routed through fiction*. Characters are a delivery vehicle for real feeling, not a substitute for it. The rule is "be emotionally true," not "be autobiographical."
- **"Check your ego / serve the artist" (Sia, Finneas, Tedder `[S20][S7][S18]`) vs. "strong singular voice / no yes-men" (Sia admitted she may have been "too dominant" with Adele; Kendrick's camp has none `[S10]`).** *Resolution:* the *producer* checks ego to serve the artist's voice; the *artist's inner circle* protects that voice from dilution. Ego goes down the chain, not up.
- **"Write the song alone first" (Finneas, Imagine Dragons) vs. "write fresh in-room with trusted producers" (Imagine Dragons *scrapped 150 demos to write in-room* `[S40]`).** *Resolution:* even the solo-daily camp rewrites the playbook periodically to break autopilot. No workflow stays optimal forever — deliberately inverting your own method is itself a method.

## Part F — Limitations & open questions (uncertainty layer)

- **Survivorship bias is severe.** Every workflow here is from a *winner*. The same moves are used by thousands who failed identically. Read these as *necessary, not sufficient*.
- **Co-writing dominates.** Per the Hit Songs Deconstructed data, modern #1s are built by benches. Any "solo genius" framing here is a simplification — Drake's reference-writer system and Sia's "throw spaghetti at the wall" model openly admit it `[S20][S29]`.
- **Artists post-rationalize.** A workflow interview is a reconstruction, sometimes flattering. Treat quotes as *what they do now*, not necessarily *what made the original hit*.
- **Recency caveat on two figures:** Max Martin and Sia are **less current** as personal chart forces than the Kendrick/SZA/Wallen/Rodrigo cohort — but their *methods* are the most-cited and evergreen, and Martin still charts via collaborators (Ariana, Taylor). I included them on method-merit; flag if you want strictly last-5-years-only.
- **Chart structure shifts in real time.** The 2024 data shows the "fast chorus" rule bending and a hip-hop/country surge (hip-hop/rap 38% of 2024 #1s — first time since 2018; country 25%; Shaboozey's *"A Bar Song"* tied the record at 19 weeks #1) `[S38]`. Any "always" rule has an expiry date — re-check against current chart analytics before betting on one.
- **Open question:** No one here fully documents the *melody-generation* moment itself — they all describe the *surrounding system* (concept-first, volume, intake, iteration). The actual flash of an undeniable melody remains the least-taught, least-explained part. The honest conclusion: **you can systematize everything around the flash; you can only maximize exposure to the flash.**
